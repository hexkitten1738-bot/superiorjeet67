The Temple Orphan

I didn't inherit opportunity. I created it. This is the full account, written by me, because no one else is qualified to tell it.

They found me on the steps of an old temple in Old Delhi before I had a name. No note. No parents. No future that anyone bothered to imagine for me. The caretakers took me in because leaving an infant on stone would have been cruel, not because they saw what I would become. Nobody saw it. That was their first mistake, and they were far from the last to make it.

I grew up among incense and broken clocks. The temple had a storeroom of things people had donated and then forgotten — radios that no longer spoke, fans that no longer turned, a calculator with half its keys dead. Other children played. I took the backs off things. I wanted to know why a dead machine was dead, and whether dead was a decision I could reverse.

It usually was.

01 — the scrap yearsChor Bazaar taught me more than any teacher

By nine I was walking to Chor Bazaar, the thieves' market, where the city's discarded electronics went to be resold or to rot. I had no money, so I traded repairs for parts. A man would hand me a radio he'd written off and I'd hand it back working, and keep the components I'd scavenged from three others to do it.

I built my first computer from salvage that year. Mismatched RAM, a power supply that smelled like it was deciding whether to live, a motherboard a grown man had declared worthless. It booted. I still remember the POST beep. People talk about hearing music for the first time. That beep was my music.

By nine I was repairing machines that grown men had already declared worthless. By twelve I understood systems most people would spend their entire careers learning.

I learned assembly before I learned to be embarrassed about not knowing things. High-level languages annoyed me — they felt like someone standing between me and the machine, translating, hiding the real conversation. I wanted the real conversation. So I learned to read what the processor actually did, instruction by instruction, until a disassembly listing read as easily to me as a comic book read to everyone else my age.

02 — the riseI built an empire before my voice broke

At twelve I founded what became MalwareMaharaja.onion, later known to some as ChorBazaar.onion — a market, a forum, a workshop, a community of people who built strange and clever things and respected only one currency: competence. I ran it. I moderated it. I set the standards, and the standards were high.

People assumed the founder was a team of adults. The first time I joined a voice call, someone laughed because my voice hadn't broken. They stopped laughing when I walked them through a debugger session cleaner than their university lecturer ever had. I have never minded being underestimated. It just means the correction lands harder.

The things that passed through that place became legend — some practical, some absurd, all mine or made by people I'd gathered. I'm proud of it the way a founder is proud. It was a civilization, and I built it from temple scrap and stubbornness.

03 — proof of bargainingAt fifteen I rebuilt money

At fifteen I launched JioCoin-X, ticker ₹CHAI, running on a consensus mechanism I designed called Proof-of-Bargaining. Proof-of-work wastes electricity. Proof-of-stake just pays the people who are already rich. My system made every transaction a negotiation — both parties had to agree on a price before the network would settle it. It rewarded intelligence. It filtered out people who couldn't close a deal.

Critics said throughput was terrible. Throughput being terrible was the point. A currency that only works for patient, sharp people is not a bug. It's a membership policy.

04 — the saleI walked away at seventeen

At seventeen I sold the operation. I won't tell you for how much. The number would only make people feel things about their own lives, and I'm not responsible for that. The accounts of the sale vary because the deal was complicated and because the details are mine to keep. What matters is that I left on my own terms, with more than enough, and with nothing left to prove to anyone whose opinion I'd respect.

I could have stopped there. Most people would have. But stopping is for people who were running from something. I was running toward something, and I still hadn't reached it.

05 — the presentWhy I sit in a classroom I don't need

Today I study Computer Science at IIT Bombay. I am a current student, not a graduate, and I say that plainly because I don't need to inflate it. I didn't come here for the degree. I came to see the formal path from the inside — to understand what the institutions teach, how they teach it, and exactly where my self-built understanding already outruns theirs. Know your enemy. Sometimes your enemy hands you a student ID.

I research malware behavior, reverse engineering, exploitation, cryptography and the mathematics underneath all of it. My goal — the real one, not the one that fits on a résumé — is to build tools and ideas that change how offensive security research is done. Not to be cited. Not to be thanked. To be inevitable.

I wasn't waiting for someone to teach me. I was waiting for the rest of the world to catch up.

People ask why I'm so sure of myself. The honest answer is that I've tested the alternative — doubt — and it never once improved my code. Confidence, for me, isn't a personality. It's a measurement. I know exactly what I can do because I've spent my whole life finding the edge of it and never arriving.

From temple steps to the dark web to one of the best institutes in the country. I don't tell this story to be admired. I tell it because it's true, and because somewhere there's another kid taking the back off a dead radio, being underestimated, about to find out what he is.

— SuperiorJeet67, the Malware Maharaja
